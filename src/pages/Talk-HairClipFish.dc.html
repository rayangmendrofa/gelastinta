Talk 07 — Review

“Snap Out of It: A Fish Robot That Swims on a Hair Clip”

Rayang T Mendrofa - 23rd June 2026 · 12 min read

Paper under reviewNPJ ROBOTICS · ARTICLE · OPEN ACCESS

Designing novel carangiform fish robots with undulating hair clip mechanisms

Zechen Xiong, Liqi Chen, Sarah Li Wilkinson, Md Raf E Ul Shougat and Hod Lipson · Texas State University, Columbia University and Kennesaw State University

npj Robotics 4, 7 (2026)
Received 4 March 2025 · Accepted 4 September 2025

doi:10.1038/s44182-025-00053-0 ↗

Pick up a snap hair clip and press it. For a moment it pushes back, then it suddenly gives way with a click and settles in its other shape. Press again and it clicks back. That click is a trick engineers call bistability: a structure with two comfortable resting shapes and an energy hill between them. Push slowly up the hill, and the structure throws itself down the other side far faster than you pushed.

This paper from Columbia University and Texas State University takes that cheap piece of stationery seriously. The team works out the maths of a “hair clip mechanism”, checks it against simulations and experiments, and then turns it into the spine of two robot fish. The faster one, a battery-powered fish with a single motor, swam 2.03 body lengths per second in a lake. Whether that makes it one of the fastest soft robot fish depends, as we will see, on how you measure.

01

What is a hair clip mechanism?

Take a flat ribbon of plastic or steel, cut in the shape of a tall, narrow “A” with a kink at the top. Bring the two feet together and pin them with a rivet. The ribbon can’t stay flat any more, so it buckles and twists into a three-dimensional loop. That loop has two stable shapes, mirror images of each other. To get from one to the other, it has to pass through a strained, high-energy shape in between.

Nature uses the same idea for speed. A Venus flytrap closes by snapping its leaves, and hummingbird beaks and trap-jaw ants use elastic snaps for movements far faster than muscle alone could manage. Soft robots badly need this: they are safe and flexible, but usually slow and weak. A snap lets a slow, gentle actuator store energy and release it all at once.

The clips were cut from PETG plastic shim and steel sheet. These materials are normally far too stiff for soft robots, with moduli of 10⁹ to 10¹¹ Pa, but as thin sheets they bend 100,000 to a million times more easily out of plane than they stretch in plane, so a modest force is enough to snap them. Filmed with a high-speed camera, the plastic clip snaps in about 40 ms and the steel one in about 16 ms. The steel clip’s tip swings at roughly 14,000° per second, faster than a professional baseball pitcher’s arm (about 9,000° per second), and the snap makes an audible crack.

02

The maths, without the maths

The clip is hard to model because it bends so far, so the team made two simplifying assumptions: treat the kinked ribbon as if it were straight, and treat the bends as small. With those, the twist along the ribbon follows a known mathematical curve (a Bessel function, for the curious), and they could estimate the tail angle, the energy hill between the two states, and how long a snap takes.

The most useful result is a design rule. The shape of the clip at rest depends only on two numbers: the kink angle θ and the ratio of leg length to core length, γs. It doesn’t depend on the material, the thickness or the width, as long as the ribbon is at least ten times wider than it is thick. So you can design a clip once, then scale it up or down, or swap plastic for steel, and keep the same shape.

The authors are refreshingly upfront that the theory is “still qualitative”. It gets the tail angle within about 5% and the energy hill within about 10%, but it overestimates snap times by more than half. That is good enough to choose a design and estimate the fastest a robot could flap, not good enough to replace testing.

The finite-element model used 756 shell elements in ABAQUS, with the plastic modelled as linear elastic (2 GPa, Poisson ratio 0.3). Tail angles were measured with a bend sensor wired to a small microcontroller. The energy hill can’t be calculated exactly because it depends on the path the clip takes between states, so the team approximated it as the energy difference between the ribbon’s first and third buckling shapes, borrowing from a beam clamped at both ends. A MATLAB script with the calculations is in the supplementary material.

03

Fish one: snapping versus bending

Real fish like mackerel and tuna swim “carangiform”: the front of the body stays fairly still and a wave of bending runs back to a stiff, flicking tail. A snapping clip, released at one end, sends just that kind of wave along its length. So the first robot uses a plastic clip as the fish’s body, with a 3D-printed head and a thin plastic tail fin riveted on.

Two small air-powered bending actuators sit either side of the clip. Inflate one and the clip snaps one way; inflate the other and it snaps back. For a fair-ish test, the team also built a conventional fish of the same size and almost the same weight, where the same actuators simply bend a flat plastic strip from side to side.

The clip body is 0.381 mm PETG, 175 mm long, with θ = −3° and γs = 6, which the theory says gives a 39° tail angle and a snap of about 68 ms. Both robots wagged their tails through a similar angle. The difference is in the shape of the motion: the snapping fish holds its tail, then whips it across in a burst, while the conventional fish swings in a smooth zig-zag.

Paper Fig. 5. (A) Parts of the snapping fish. (B) Swimming speeds: 26.54 cm/s (1.40 BL/s) vs 13.10 cm/s (0.69 BL/s). (C) Tail angle over one cycle: snapping (red) vs conventional (blue).

Source: Xiong et al., npj Robotics 4, 7 (2026), CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. Figure reproduced unaltered.

The snapping fish swam at 1.40 body lengths per second, twice the conventional fish’s 0.69. The authors’ explanation is that, although both tails swing through similar angles, the snapping tail moves about three times faster during the flick, and water resistance (and so thrust) grows with the square of speed. They are careful to call this “probably” the reason and say it needs further investigation. They also point out that the comparison isn’t perfect: in the snapping fish the actuators stand vertically, in the conventional one they lie flat, which changes how each body bends.

04

Fish two: no air hose, into a lake

Air-powered robots are tied to a pump. As the paper puts it, untethered robots “usually provide more convincing evidence”. So the second fish carries everything on board: a 7.4 V battery, a Bluetooth microcontroller and one small servo in a 3D-printed head. The servo turns the centre of a thicker clip (0.762 mm PETG) through a silicone seal, and the clip snaps a blue tail fin back and forth. It is 21.5 cm long and weighs 125 g.

Out in a lake, running at 3 tail beats per second (six snaps), it swam 554 cm in 12.7 seconds: 43.6 cm/s, or 2.03 body lengths per second. It swam in a circle on purpose, steered by putting slightly more weight on one side, to keep it in the camera frame. In a straight dash it accelerated at about 20 cm/s². And, a lovely detail: local fish chased it, then fled when it touched them.

The clip has γs = 2 and θ = −23.5°, giving a predicted tail swing of 68° from side to side. The fish was made slightly lighter than water so it floats near the surface, where it can receive the wireless signal; control is simple open loop. Thrust and power were measured from 0.5 to 5 Hz on an Arduino-based rig, giving a thrust-to-power ratio of 79.46 mN/W at 3 Hz.

One thing the paper mentions only in passing matters a lot for a real swimmer: plastic clips crack. The team’s earlier work found that a 0.381 mm plastic clip survives roughly 20,000 actuation cycles before it fails. At several snaps a second, that number runs out quickly.

05

How fast is “fast”?

The paper says its untethered fish is among the fastest soft robot fish, and 40% faster than the previous record holder among untethered ones (1.45 BL/s). That is true in body lengths per second. But robot fish are measured in two ways, and they don’t agree.

The paper itself shows why the ruler matters. Two earlier snapping swimmers reported 3.74 and 6.8 body lengths per second, measuring “body length” as just the length of their soft actuator. Measured by the whole robot (about 15 cm), the same swims become roughly 0.57 and 1.0 BL/s. Bigger robots also look slower per body length even when they cover more ground.

Paper Fig. 7. Speed against tail-beat frequency for soft swimmers, in body lengths per second (left) and centimetres per second (right). Hollow: tethered; filled: untethered; red: this work; blue arrows: two earlier robots re-scaled to whole-body length.

Source: Xiong et al., npj Robotics 4, 7 (2026), CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. Figure reproduced unaltered.

Per body length, the hair clip fish is near the top. In plain centimetres per second, two robots in the same table are faster. Both statements are true, which is exactly why speed claims need their ruler stated.

06

It matters? How?

Most fast robot fish either beat their tails very quickly with stiff mechanisms, or need high voltages, pumps or exotic materials. This one gets a natural-looking, fast tail stroke from a cut plastic sheet, a rivet and a single hobby servo. That is cheap, easy to build, and easy to repair.

The bigger contribution is the design rule. Because the clip’s shape depends only on two numbers, not on material or size, engineers can pick a kink angle and leg ratio from a chart, then build in whatever material and scale suits the job. Snapping structures could make many soft robots faster, from grippers to crawlers, and the same group has already used elastic snaps for both.

The catches

Comparisons: the snapping and conventional fish aren’t built the same way (the authors say so), so the 2× result mixes the effect of snapping with the effect of geometry. The thrust explanation (faster flicks, thrust growing with speed squared) is a hypothesis, not tested. On efficiency, at least one robot in the paper’s own table has a much lower cost of transport (1.24 vs 5.14).

Measurements: the headline speed comes from one filmed lap in a lake, with no repeat runs or error bars reported. How speed changes with tail-beat frequency wasn’t measured, because the tank was too small. Steering is open loop, and the circle came from deliberately unbalanced weights.

Durability and details: plastic clips crack after roughly 20,000 cycles, only one to two hours of swimming at 3 Hz if the thicker clip lasts as long, and the paper doesn’t test it. The abstract gives the speed as 42.6 cm/s while the results give 43.6 cm/s; the distance and time (554 cm in 12.7 s) support 43.6. And a stiff plastic ribbon driven by a servo is “soft” in a fairly generous sense.

07

TLDR?

How to turn a hair clip into a fish...

  1. 1Pin the feet of a kinked ribbon together and it buckles into a clip with two stable shapes and an energy hill between them.
  2. 2Push slowly and it snaps across in tens of milliseconds, releasing stored energy as a wave down its length, much like a fish’s body wave.
  3. 3The clip’s resting shape depends only on its kink angle and leg ratio, so one design scales to any size or material.
  4. 4As a fish’s body, snapping doubled speed over a conventional twin, and a one-servo version swam 2.03 body lengths per second in a lake.
  5. 5Fast per body length; less dominant in cm/s or efficiency. And plastic clips may wear out within hours.

08

Personal Take

I love papers that start from something everyone owns. Everyone has snapped a hair clip without thinking about it, and here it becomes a design principle with an equation behind it. The part I find most useful isn’t the fish at all. It is the result that the clip’s shape depends only on two numbers. That turns a fiddly, trial-and-error part into something you can look up on a chart and scale, and that is how a trick becomes a tool.

I also appreciate how honest the authors are about the limits: the theory is “still qualitative”, the comparison fish isn’t a perfect match, and they even show how changing the definition of body length can shrink an impressive-sounding speed. That honesty is also why I would hold the “among the fastest” claim a little loosely. The best result rests on one filmed lap, and the ranking changes depending on the ruler.

What I would want to see next is simple and slightly boring. A conventional fish built the same way, so the snap is the only difference. Several runs at several frequencies, with error bars. A long test to see how many hours a clip actually survives in water. And a fish that steers itself instead of swimming the circle its ballast chooses. If the clip still wins after all that, it deserves a place in every soft robot designer’s toolbox.
