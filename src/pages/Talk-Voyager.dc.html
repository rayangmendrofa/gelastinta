A Day Away. Voyager 1 Is Now One Light Day from Home

Rayang T Mendrofa · 20th November 2026 · 16 min read

Launched 5 September 1977 · 49 years, 2 months and 11 days in flight

Behind the title is the cover of the Golden Record that Voyager 1 carries. Photo NASA/JPL, public domain.

On Wednesday, at 17:16 in the afternoon Jakarta time, a spacecraft from 1977 crossed a line that nobody can see. Nothing on board changed. No alarm went off. But from that moment, anything Voyager 1 says to us needs more than a full day to arrive. Say hello at the crossing and the earliest possible answer lands today, Friday 20 November, at 17:16.

Voyager 1 left Earth on 5 September 1977 to fly past Jupiter and Saturn. Forty nine years, two months and 11 days later it is about 25.9 billion kilometres away, the first human made object to reach one light day from home. It still sends data, slowly, with two of its ten science instruments switched on and a power supply that shrinks by about 4 watts a year. This talk is my small party for it.

01

What’s the milestone?

A light day is a distance, not a time. It is how far light travels in 24 hours, about 25.9 billion kilometres, or 173 times the distance from Earth to the Sun. Radio is light too, just at a wavelength our eyes cannot see, so a radio message to Voyager 1 now spends a whole day in flight. A command from Earth arrives a day later, and the reply needs another day to come back. Suzanne Dodd, the Voyager project manager at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL), put it plainly. Once you send a message, you wait two days for the answer.

The team gave the moment to the second, 10:16:07 UTC on 18 November 2026. That was 5:16 in the morning in Florida, where Voyager launched, and 17:16 in the afternoon in Jakarta. The distance is measured from Earth, not from the Sun. Earth swings round the Sun once a year, sometimes moving towards Voyager and sometimes away, so the gap between them wobbles a little on top of its steady growth. News stories over the past year gave dates from 13 to 18 November before the team confirmed the exact moment.

Nothing physical happens at a light day, and JPL says so. The spacecraft does not feel it and the space around it does not change. It is a milestone the way a birthday is, a round number we picked. But it is a very good round number, because it turns a distance too big to picture into something everyone knows. A day.

Voyager 2, the twin, took the long way past Uranus and Neptune and is further back. It is not expected to reach a light day until November 2035, and even hopeful estimates say it will probably have gone quiet by then.

    Light times and distances from NASA’s light day explainer. The last column is my own arithmetic, distance divided by Voyager 1’s present speed of about 61,000 km/h.

    Light moves at exactly 299,792.458 kilometres per second, a number fixed by definition. Multiply by the 86,400 seconds in a day and a light day comes to 25,902,068,371 kilometres. NASA rounds it to about 26 billion kilometres, or 16 billion miles, and other NASA pages say 25.9 billion.

    An astronomical unit (AU) is the average distance from Earth to the Sun, 149,597,870.7 km. Divide and a light day is about 173.1 AU. A light year is 365.25 light days, about 9.46 trillion kilometres, so Proxima Centauri at 4.24 light years is roughly 1,550 light days away.

    The Voyager column divides each distance by 61,000 km/h. Voyager 1 did not travel at one steady speed, though. It slowed as it climbed away from the Sun and sped up when it swung past Jupiter and Saturn, so its real trip to a light day took 49 years rather than the 48 the plain division gives.

    Source: NASA Science, What is a light day? (November 2026). Arithmetic mine.

    02

    So, how did it get there?

    Three things got Voyager 1 this far. A rare line up of the planets, a power supply that never needs the Sun, and a team on Earth that keeps a 1970s computer running from a day away.

    Voyager 1 launched from Cape Canaveral on 5 September 1977, two weeks after its twin, on a Titan IIIE Centaur rocket. The timing came from a line up of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune that happens about once every 175 years. It let a spacecraft use each planet’s gravity to bend its path and pick up speed for the next one, a trick called a gravity assist.

    Voyager 1 took the faster route. It passed Jupiter on 5 March 1979 and Saturn on 12 November 1980. At Saturn the team chose a close pass of Titan, Saturn’s largest moon, over a path on to Uranus and Neptune. That flyby flung Voyager 1 north, out of the flat plane the planets orbit in, and it has coasted outwards on the same path ever since. Today it moves at about 61,000 km/h, roughly 17 kilometres every second.

    Past two giants, then up and away

    Out there the Sun is far too faint for solar panels. Voyager runs on radioisotope thermoelectric generators, RTGs, which turn the heat of decaying plutonium into electricity. The plutonium slowly runs down and the parts that convert its heat age too, so each Voyager loses about 4 watts of power every year. That is about what a small LED bulb uses. On a machine with this little to spare, every 4 watts is a decision.

    Voyager 1 launched with ten science instruments. Some finished their planet work, some wore out, and the rest are being switched off one by one to save power.

    To buy more time, the team tried something bold this year. In early September they swapped three devices on Voyager 1 for lower power versions in a single operation they nicknamed the Big Bang. The six devices involved help keep the inside of the spacecraft warm, so all three swaps had to happen together. They had done the same on Voyager 2 about six weeks earlier. Without it, another instrument could have been switched off as early as the summer of 2027.

    Every word goes through NASA’s Deep Space Network, giant radio dishes in California, near Madrid and near Canberra, spread round the globe so one of them can always see the right patch of sky. In late October the Canberra antenna tracked Voyager 1 at 25.80 billion km.

    The delay shapes how the team works. On 14 November 2023 Voyager 1 started sending back data that made no sense. Engineers traced it to one memory chip in the flight data subsystem (FDS), the computer that packages science and engineering data before it is sent home. About 3 % of that computer’s memory was corrupted. Nobody can swap a chip 24 billion kilometres away, so they rewrote the software around it and moved the affected code to other parts of memory. The first patch went up on 18 April 2024. The signal needed 22.5 hours each way, so the team gathered on 20 April to see whether it had worked. It had.

    The surprises have not stopped. On 18 and 19 October 2026 the Deep Space Network missed two scheduled contacts with Voyager 1. The signal came back on 20 October. One set of thrusters had stopped working and the spacecraft had switched itself to its backups, part of its fault protection, which can change to spare hardware without waiting for Earth. NASA has not found the cause yet. The mission manager, Kareem Badaruddin, said the team has to expect the unexpected at this stage.

      04

      Earth, 0.12 of a pixel

      Home from about 6 billion km away, the Pale Blue Dot.

      Two giants in 20 months

      At Jupiter, Voyager 1 found a faint ring, two small new moons named Thebe and Metis, and the first active volcanoes seen anywhere beyond Earth, erupting on the moon Io. Twenty months later it reached Saturn. There it passed Titan at a distance of about 4,000 km and found a thick atmosphere of nitrogen with methane and other carbon compounds, the kind of chemistry that made NASA wonder whether reactions that come before life might be possible there.

      One last look home

      On 14 February 1990, about 6 billion km from the Sun, Voyager 1 turned its camera back and took 60 frames of the planets, a family portrait of the solar system. In one frame Earth covers 0.12 of a pixel, a pale speck sitting in a streak of sunlight scattered inside the camera. Thirty four minutes later the cameras were switched off for good, to save power for the long trip ahead. The astronomer Carl Sagan, who had pushed for years to take the picture, called it the Pale Blue Dot. The version below is NASA’s 2020 remaster, reprocessed with modern software while keeping to the original data.

      The Pale Blue Dot Revisited. Earth is the tiny speck about halfway down, inside the band of light on the right. Every person who has ever lived is in that dot.

      Image: NASA/JPL-Caltech, Pale Blue Dot Revisited (2020), from Voyager 1 data of 14 February 1990. Public domain.

      Out past the Sun’s bubble

      The Sun blows a constant wind of charged particles, and that wind carves a bubble called the heliosphere in the thin gas between the stars. Its outer edge, where the solar wind finally gives way, is the heliopause. Voyager 1 crossed it in August 2012, at about 121 AU from the Sun, and became the first human made object in interstellar space. Voyager 2 followed in November 2018. Between them they are the only spacecraft that have measured interstellar space from the inside. The two instruments still on keep at it. The magnetometer reads the magnetic field out there, and the plasma wave instrument picks up waves rippling through the thin plasma around the spacecraft.

      A record for whoever finds it

      Both Voyagers carry a 12 inch, gold plated copper record, the disc at the top of this page. It is a message for anyone who might find the spacecraft one day, chosen by a committee chaired by Carl Sagan. It holds 116 images, natural sounds, spoken greetings in 55 languages and 27 pieces of music. The cover shows how to play it and a map that places the Sun among 14 pulsars. A small patch of uranium 238 is plated on the cover too. Half of it decays every 4.468 billion years, so whoever finds it can tell how long it has been travelling.

      Indonesia is on it twice. One of the 27 pieces is Puspawarna, Kinds of Flowers, a Javanese gamelan piece played by the court gamelan of Paku Alaman in Yogyakarta and recorded in 1971 by the ethnomusicologist Robert E. Brown. And one of the 55 greetings is in Indonesian. “Selamat malam hadirin sekalian, selamat berpisah dan sampai bertemu lagi di lain waktu.” NASA translates it as good night, ladies and gentlemen, goodbye and see you next time.

      04

      It matters? How?

      For engineers, Voyager is the longest reliability test ever run. It was built for a tour of Jupiter and Saturn that wrapped up in 1980. Forty six years later it is still answering, on computers designed before the first IBM PC went on sale. Part of that is luck. Most of it is margin. The designers left spare thrusters, backup hardware and fault protection that can switch to them without asking Earth, and last month that is exactly what happened when a set of thrusters stopped.

      It is also a lesson in working with what you have. The people running Voyager today cannot add memory, cannot replace a part and cannot get an answer in less than two days. They patch software around dead chips, swap old devices for lower power ones and choose which instrument to switch off next. Most engineering is about making things work. Theirs is about making a machine stop working as slowly and as usefully as possible.

      And it gives us a ruler for the universe. Our fastest way out of the solar system so far needed almost half a century to cover one light day. The nearest star after the Sun is about 1,550 light days away, roughly 75,000 years at Voyager’s pace. Voyager 1 is not even heading there. NASA says it will pass within about 1.7 light years of a star called AC +79 3888, also known as Gliese 445, in about 40,000 years, long after it has gone silent.

      The catches

      A light day is a symbol. Nothing changes on board. The moment is set by the distance from Earth, which shifts with Earth’s orbit, so it is a line we chose to measure rather than something the spacecraft notices.

      Most numbers here are rounded. NASA’s own pages round the light day to 25.9 or 26 billion km and give Voyager’s speed as about 61,000 km/h or 17 km/s. My own sums use those rounded values, so read them as approximate.

      The recent part of the story is still unfolding. The cause of the October thruster fault is unknown, and the Big Bang and the October outage come from JPL’s November update rather than a peer reviewed paper.

      The end is coming. Every year the power drops and another trade has to be made. NASA does not promise a date for the last instrument to go dark, and neither will I.

      06

      Personal Take

      Of all the numbers in this story, the one I keep coming back to is 4 watts. That is about what a small LED bulb uses, and every year Voyager loses that much and someone at JPL has to decide what to switch off next. Most of my engineering life has been about making things work. These people spend their days deciding, very carefully, how a machine should slowly stop working, from a day away, on computers older than the IBM PC. I find that a little sad and incredibly cool at the same time.

      What I did not know before I started this talk is that Indonesia is on the Golden Record twice. A gamelan piece from Yogyakarta, and a greeting in our own language. Of all the things someone could say to strangers between the stars, ours is a polite goodbye at the end of an evening. Selamat malam, sampai bertemu lagi. Somehow that feels very Indonesian to me.

      As a former international student, I know a little about messages that land while everyone at home is asleep. Voyager’s take a whole day, and the team still waits up for every one of them. I also made a short video for this one, linked at the top, if you would rather watch than read.

      So happy light day, Voyager 1. Selamat malam, and see you next time.
