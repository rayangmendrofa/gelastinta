01 — The story

A brain you can switch on

In my talk The Whole Fly, Wired I reviewed three papers on the fruit-fly connectome. One of them, by Philip Shiu and colleagues (Nature, 2024), turns the wiring map of a whole fly brain into a computer model that can be switched on. This hobby project runs that published model on a laptop and asks it one question: what happens inside the brain when the fly tastes sugar?

The page follows the experiment in four steps. First the map, then the simple rule every neuron follows. Then the main test: switch on the 21 sugar-taste neurons the paper used and follow the spikes to MN9, a motor neuron that lifts the proboscis, the fly’s feeding tube. Then silence two neurons out of 127,400, run the exact same experiment again, and plug the brain into a simple walking fly to see what that difference could look like.

Nothing here was recorded from a living fly. Every spike is computed by the model and replayed from runs I made on 23 October 2026.

Where it started: my review of the three papers →

Credit where it’s due

I did not build the brain model or the wiring map. The model is the work of Philip Shiu, Gabriella Sterne, Nico Spiller and colleagues in Kristin Scott’s lab, shared openly under the MIT licence. The map is the FlyWire connectome. My part is running the model, checking it against the original, and showing what it does.

02 — The map DATA

Every neuron, in its place

The FlyWire project reconstructed the whole brain of one adult female fruit fly from electron-microscope images: every neuron, and every synapse between them. The release used here (v630) has 127,400 neurons joined by about 14.7 million connections, made of 52.8 million synapses.

Each dot below is one of those neurons, placed where it sits in the real brain (positions from Schlegel et al. 2024, matched by neuron ID; 127,320 of the 127,400 have one). More than half of them are in the two optic lobes, the wide wings on either side, which handle vision. Taste and feeding live in the lower middle, in the subesophageal zone.

03 — Turning the map into a brain MODEL

Every neuron is a leaky bucket

Shiu and colleagues gave all 127,400 neurons the same simple rule, called leaky integrate-and-fire. Think of a bucket with a small hole. Incoming spikes pour charge in, the charge slowly leaks out, and if the level rises past a line the neuron fires a spike of its own and the bucket empties.

How hard one neuron pushes another depends only on how many synapses connect them, and on whether its chemical messenger excites or inhibits (GABA and glutamate count as inhibitory). Each synapse is worth 0.275 mV, the model’s one tuned number. There is no learning, no hormones and no spontaneous firing. That is the whole model.

The rule, as the model writes it

dv/dt = (v₀ − v + g) / τ_m
dg/dt = −g / τ_s
v > v_th  →  spike, v = v₀, g = 0
input spike  →  g += synapses × 0.275 mV
Resting level v₀−52 mV
Firing line v_th−45 mV
Leak time τ_m20 ms
Input decay τ_s5 ms
Rest after a spike2.2 ms
Travel delay1.8 ms
Time step0.1 ms

04 — Sugar in, spikes out MODEL

Follow one second of sugar through the brain

This is the paper’s own test. The 21 sugar-taste neurons (gustatory receptor neurons) get random spikes at 100 per second each for one simulated second, a stand-in for the fly touching sugar, and every spike anywhere in the brain is recorded.

The readout is MN9. Its firing drives the proboscis drawing beside the brain; how far a real proboscis moves for a given MN9 rate is not known, so that mapping is my choice and tagged APP. Switch to the second run to see the same input with two neurons silenced.

05 — Silence two neurons MODEL

Two neurons out of 127,400

The model can switch any neuron off. Silencing, exactly as in the original code, removes everything a neuron sends: it can still fire, but nothing downstream hears it. I silenced Roundup, a cell type the paper places just before the motor neurons in the sugar-to-feeding pathway (two neurons, one per side), and repeated the same 10 trials.

The comparison is paired. Trial 3 of the silenced run received exactly the same random sugar input as trial 3 of the normal run, so each pair of bars differs only by the silencing.

Result, within this model

MN9: 65.5 → 24.7 spikes/s

A drop of 62%, lower in all 10 of 10 paired trials. The other MN9 fell harder, from 49.8 to 5.4 (−89%).

Does it match the paper?

Yes in direction. The paper calls Roundup “the pre-MN with the strongest predicted silencing phenotype”. This is a result inside the model, not a tested prediction about a living fly.

06 — From brain to behaviour HYBRID DEMO

Give the brain a body (a pretend one)

The brain model only produces spikes. To see what they could mean for a fly, I connected it to a simple, hand-written walking controller in a small arena with two sugar patches. The fly wanders at random. When it stands on sugar, the 21 sugar neurons switch on. If MN9 then fires fast enough, the controller stops the fly to feed.

The walking and turning are mine, not the model’s: the wiring map has no nerve cord, so the brain cannot move legs. Only the decision to stop and feed comes from the simulated brain. Nothing learns, and a fly that finds sugar found it by chance.

07 — Under the hood

How the project works

Python for the brain, TypeScript for the pictures

The project, which I call FlyBrain Arcade, is a small pipeline. It fetches the original model and its data, runs the whole brain fast enough to experiment with, measures the results honestly, and turns them into the pictures on this page. Pick a stage to see what it does and which files do it.

Stage 01 DATA

Fetch the original, untouched

Downloads the published model at a pinned version (commit 91bdd1e) together with its FlyWire v630 files, about 200 MB, and checks each file’s SHA-256 fingerprint. The model’s parameters are read straight from the original code rather than typed in again, and a test checks them.

scripts/fetch_upstream.pyflybrain/upstream.py

Stage 02 DATA

Turn 14.7 million connections into a fast lookup

Loads the connection table into a compressed sparse matrix: for each neuron, the list of neurons it talks to and how strongly (synapse count × ±1 × 0.275 mV). A second file holds the verified neuron IDs: the 21 sugar neurons, both MN9s, Roundup and the 106 feeding-region cell types from the paper’s own files.

flybrain/network.pyflybrain/neurons.py

Stage 03 MODEL

Step the whole brain forward, 0.1 ms at a time

Two engines run the same model on all 127,400 neurons, with nothing pruned. The default is my Numba re-implementation, which compiles the update loop to machine code: one simulated second takes about 2.3 s on my laptop. The second engine runs the unmodified original Brian2 code, about 21 s per simulated second, and serves as the reference.

flybrain/engine_fast.pyflybrain/engine_brian2.py

Stage 04 MODEL

Switch neurons on, or off

Switching on uses the original mechanism: each target neuron gets random kicks of 68.75 mV at the chosen rate, enough to make it fire. Switching off sets all of a neuron’s outgoing connections to zero, exactly like the original silence(). Settings are checked before a run: 0–200 spikes/s, up to 2 s and up to 30 trials.

flybrain/experiment.py

Stage 05 MODEL

Count spikes, compare fairly

Computes spike counts, firing rates and rate-over-time curves in 5 ms bins. Comparisons are paired: trial k of both runs uses the same random seed, so a difference comes from the intervention alone. Every result exports as CSV, JSON, SVG or PNG with a record of exactly how it was made.

flybrain/metrics.pyflybrain/runs.py

Stage 06 APP

Close the loop every 50 ms

Every 50 ms of simulated time: sugar under the fly sets the sugar neurons’ input rate, the brain runs, MN9’s rate becomes a stop-and-feed signal, and the hand-written controller moves the fly. The brain keeps its full state between windows, and a test proves that chopping a run into windows changes not a single spike.

flybrain/arena/

Stage 07 APP

Draw it

A FastAPI server runs simulations in a background worker. The React and three.js app draws a 3D fly, the brain cloud with spike flashes and the arena; the frame at the top of this page comes from it. One script exports the precomputed runs that this page replays, so it needs no server.

flybrain/api.pyflybrain/jobs.pyfrontend/src/three/scripts/export_site_data.py

Stage 08

Prove it matches the original

The same random input goes into the original Brian2 code and into my Numba version, and every spike of all 127,400 neurons is compared. They are identical in all four test runs. Against the results bundled with the paper’s code, firing rates agree with a correlation above 0.999. 44 automated tests pass.

scripts/validate_exact.pyscripts/validate_statistical.pybackend/tests/

08 — Honest limits

What this does and doesn’t show

  • Everything moving here is computed by a model. None of it is a recording from a living fly.
  • The model leaves a lot out: hormones and other neuromodulators, electrical synapses, neuron shapes, the body and learning. Its neurons never fire on their own.
  • Absolute firing rates are uncertain. The paper tested the model mainly on whether switching neurons on or off changes the output in the right direction.
  • How strongly MN9 moves the proboscis is not known. The extension shown is my display choice: 0% below 5 spikes per second, 100% at 100.
  • The 3D fly in the top frame is a stylised house fly made with an AI 3D tool, not an anatomical model. The brain inside it is a fruit-fly brain.
  • In the arena, walking is a hand-written random walk. The brain map has no nerve cord, so the model cannot move legs.
  • Nothing here learns. The arena fly does not know where the food is; it only stops when the model’s feeding output is high.
