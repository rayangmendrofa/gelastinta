Talk 05 — Review

“Inception, but for Homework: Solving Puzzles Inside Your Dreams”

Rayang T Mendrofa - 23rd April 2026 · 8 min read

Paper under reviewNeuroscience of Consciousness · RESEARCH ARTICLE · OPEN ACCESS

Creative problem-solving after experimentally provoking dreams of unsolved puzzles during REM sleep

Karen R. Konkoly, Daniel J. Morris, Kaitlyn Hurka, Alysiana M. Martinez, Kristin E.G. Sanders and Ken A. Paller · Northwestern University, Dust Systems and University of Notre Dame

Neurosci. Conscious. 2026(1), niaf067
Received 1 September 2025 · Accepted 22 December 2025

doi:10.1093/nc/niaf067 ↗

Everyone has been told to “sleep on it”. Dmitri Mendeleev is said to have seen the periodic table in a dream. Paul McCartney woke up with the melody of Yesterday in his head. Mary Shelley’s Frankenstein began as a vivid night-time vision. Great stories, but they are only stories. Nobody can prove the dream did the work, rather than the hours of thinking before it or the shower after it.

A team at Northwestern University decided to stop collecting anecdotes and start steering dreams. They gave people puzzles they couldn’t solve, quietly played each puzzle’s sound while they were in REM sleep, and asked them, quite literally, to keep working on it in the dream. Then they checked who solved what the next morning.

01

What’s in the paper?

There is decent lab evidence that sleep helps with creative problems: in several studies, people are more likely to crack a puzzle after a night’s sleep than after the same time awake. The evidence that dreaming is what helps is much weaker. Correlations can’t tell you whether the dream caused the insight. And experiments that simply ask people to think about a problem before bed can’t separate the dream from the thinking.

The tool the team used is called targeted memory reactivation, or TMR. A sound that was linked to something during the day is replayed quietly during sleep, nudging the brain to revisit that memory. The twist is who they tested. Most of the 20 participants have lucid dreams at least once a month (they know they are dreaming while it happens), so they could be asked to work on a puzzle inside the dream, and even tell the experimenters, in real time, that they were doing it.

The puzzles came from a bank of 25 matchstick, rebus and spatial puzzles, chosen because they need a sudden change of approach rather than step-by-step working. Each was paired at random with one of 27 short music or nature clips: 8–12 seconds of sound, then 3–7 seconds of silence so the brain doesn’t tune it out. Before bed, participants had to name the right puzzle for each sound, four times over.

In REM sleep, cues started at the quietest volume the person could detect and paused at any sign of waking: muscle tension, alpha waves, or the eyes going still. Both puzzles got cued in 62% of REM periods; in the rest, only one did.

02

How do you talk to someone who is dreaming?

In REM sleep your body is paralysed, apart from two things: your eyes and your breathing. So that is the keyboard. Before sleep, participants agreed on two signals. Looking hard left-right-left-right means “I’m lucid”. A burst of quick sniffs means “I heard a puzzle sound and I’m working on it”. Both stand out clearly on the sensors, so the team could watch a message arrive from inside a dream while the person was still asleep.

03

Did the sounds get into the dreams?

Yes, more often than you might expect. Participants reported about two dreams a night, 71 in total, and 15 of the 20 had at least one dream about an unsolved puzzle. On average, a quarter of each person’s unsolved puzzles turned up in a dream. Sometimes directly, sometimes as something puzzle-shaped: one person was catching fish in a river with a net and felt like they were “working on something… like a puzzle”. They solved it the next morning.

Crucially, the puzzles whose sounds were played turned up far more than the ones left alone. Being lucid helped too: about a quarter of the puzzles cued during lucid dreams made it into the dream, against fewer than one in ten during ordinary dreams.

Not everyone responded the same way. Twelve participants, the authors’ “targeted dreamers”, dreamed more about cued puzzles than uncued ones: on average 43% more. The other eight “non-responders” dreamed slightly less about cued puzzles (5% less on average). Two blind raters scored every dream report, without knowing which puzzles had been cued.

Solving was analysed with mixed-effects models that account for each person’s overall ability. A puzzle appearing in a dream predicted solving it (p = .037); merely hearing the sound inside a dream did not. Across everyone, the interaction between cueing and dreaming only trended (p = .057), so the authors could not run their planned test of whether dreaming explained the effect of the sounds. The targeted-dreamer split was a follow-up analysis.

Put together: when a sound managed to steer a dream towards a puzzle, that puzzle was more likely to be solved. When people heard the sound but the puzzle itself never appeared in the dream, there was no benefit. That is a hint that the dream is doing something, not just the sound.

Trying hard, even inside a dream, may not be the best way to have an idea.

The lucid result is the twist. The authors suggest that deliberate effort may drag you back onto the same wrong approach you were stuck on while awake, while ordinary dreams let you forget it and wander towards distant associations.

05

It matters? How?

Dream research has a chicken-and-egg problem. People who dream about a task often do better at it the next day, but maybe they dream about it because they were already more engaged. This study flips the direction. By choosing at random which puzzles to play into REM sleep, the team could push dreams towards particular problems and then follow exactly those problems into the morning.

It is also a lovely piece of method. Two-way communication with a sleeping person, sounds going in and eye movements and sniffs coming out, turns the dream from something you describe over breakfast into something you can run an experiment inside.

The catches

Only 20 people and about eight unsolved puzzles each, so the study had little statistical power. Across everyone, the sounds had no reliable effect on solving. The clear benefit only appears after splitting people into targeted dreamers and non-responders, and that split was made after the data came in. The design can’t rule out that people simply thought about a dreamed-of puzzle after waking. Participants knew sounds might be played, and frequent lucid dreamers aren’t typical sleepers. And every puzzle had one right answer, so it says nothing yet about open-ended creativity.

06

TLDR?

Dream engineering, in four steps...

  1. 1People got stuck on puzzles, each with its own soundtrack, then slept in a lab.
  2. 2During REM sleep, the sounds for half the unsolved puzzles were played quietly; lucid dreamers replied with eye movements and sniffs.
  3. 3The puzzles whose sounds were played showed up in dreams far more often.
  4. 4Puzzles that made it into a dream were solved more often the next morning (42% vs 17%). Lucid effort didn’t help, and the evidence is still early.

07

Personal Take

“Sleep on it” is advice I have heard my whole life, usually as a polite way of saying “stop thinking about it for now”. What I like about this paper is that it stops treating that advice as folklore and builds an instrument to test it. As an engineer, the part that impressed me most isn’t even the result; it’s the interface. A person in REM sleep can’t speak or move, so the team built a communication protocol out of the only two channels left: the eyes and the breath. That is real-time telemetry from inside a dream.

The finding that surprised me is the lucid one. You would expect being aware inside your dream to be the superpower, the moment you can sit down and deliberately work on the problem. Instead, puzzles that appeared in ordinary dreams were solved more often, and the best rate came from dreams people had already forgotten. I find that oddly comforting. Sometimes the most useful thing your brain can do with a problem is to stop holding it so tightly.

I wouldn’t plan an exam around it yet: twenty people, a handful of puzzles each, and the clearest effect only shows up after splitting the group. But as a first step it is exactly the kind of experiment I enjoy. Take an old anecdote, design a clever rig, and make the question measurable. Next time I am stuck on a design problem, I might just go to bed.
