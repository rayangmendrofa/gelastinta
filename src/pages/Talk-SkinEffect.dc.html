Talk 13 · Review

Skin in the Game. Why Noise Can Help Light Pile Up at One Edge

Rayang T Mendrofa · 13th November 2026 · 15 min read

Paper under reviewNATURE COMMUNICATIONS · ARTICLE · OPEN ACCESS

Decoherence resilience of the non-Hermitian skin effect

Kunkun Wang, Lei Xiao, Stefano Longhi and Peng Xue · Anhui University, Southeast University, Politecnico di Milano, IFISC Palma de Mallorca

Engineers spend a lot of money fighting noise. We bolt optics to tables that float on air, wrap cables in shielding and filter every signal we can get our hands on. So when a paper says noise can make something work better, it gets my attention the way a free lunch does. You check the small print first.

The something here is a strange kind of one way traffic called the non Hermitian skin effect. Give a system the right lopsided losses and everything in it drifts towards one edge and piles up there, wherever it starts. This paper builds it with single photons taking an eight step walk across a row of 17 positions, then adds two kinds of noise on purpose, each with its own dial. One kind of noise can make the drift faster. The other either kills the drift or speeds it up, and which one you get depends only on whether it comes before or after the loss.

01

What’s in the paper?

First, the name. Most of the physics you meet at university describes closed systems. Energy stays in, and the maths has a tidy property called being Hermitian. Real things leak. A laser cavity loses light, a circuit loses charge, and once you write the leaks into the equations they stop being Hermitian. Physicists call these non Hermitian systems, and over the past decade they have turned out to do things closed systems cannot.

The skin effect is the oddest of those things. In a normal material an edge changes things near the edge and leaves the middle alone. In a system with the right one sided losses, the edge reorganises everything. Every wave the system can carry ends up squeezed against one boundary, its skin. Start some light in the middle and it does more than spread out. It walks steadily towards that edge, at a speed the paper calls the drift velocity.

That much was known for clean, coherent light. Noisy light was the open question, and noise usually wrecks wave effects. Once a wave loses track of its own phase it behaves like a crowd of classical particles bumping around at random, like the water molecules wandering through brain tissue in Talk 12. Would the drift survive that? The four authors, from Anhui University, Southeast University, Politecnico di Milano and IFISC in Palma de Mallorca, set out to measure it.

Their test bed is a photonic quantum walk. Picture a coin toss game played by a single photon. Its polarisation is the coin, horizontal (H) or vertical (V) or a mix of both. Its position, from −8 to +8, is stored in how much the light’s wavefront is twisted (its orbital angular momentum). Every step does three things. A leaky element throws away part of the vertical light, with a strength γ (gamma) between 0 and 1. A coin turns the polarisation by a fixed angle. Then a shift moves the horizontal part one place right and the vertical part one place left. The team changed how leaky each step is, plus two kinds of noise, each with a dial from 0 (none) to 1 (full).

    If you studied electrical engineering, skin effect means something else, alternating current crowding into the outer layer of a wire at high frequency. Same picture of things squeezed into the surface, different physics. The non Hermitian skin effect has nothing to do with wires.

    Here is Hermitian in plain words. A Hermitian operator describes a closed system. Its energies are real numbers and its modes are independent of one another, so nothing drains away. Allow loss and the energies become complex numbers, where the imaginary part says how fast each mode fades. The mode that fades slowest wins in the long run. In this walk the slowest fading mode is one that moves, so the surviving light moves with it.

    Decoherence is the general word for a quantum system losing its phase to the outside world. The paper uses two textbook types. Dephasing scrambles the phase between H and V without changing how much of each there is. Amplitude damping makes V relax into H, the way an excited atom drops back to its ground state, so it changes the amounts as well.

    02

    So, how did they do it?

    The noise in this experiment is made on purpose, one clean setting at a time, and mixed by the clock.

    The photons come in pairs from a nonlinear crystal called BBO. Detecting one photon of a pair tells the team that its twin has entered the walk. The twin starts in a plain, untwisted beam, which is position 0, with its coin set to an equal mix of H and V.

    Each step is built from ordinary optics. A pair of beam displacers splits the light into two parallel paths by polarisation. A half wave plate in the vertical path, turned to the right angle, throws away a share γ of that light before the paths merge again. Two more half wave plates turn the polarisation by the coin angle θ, which was π/4 (45°) in every measurement. Then a q plate, a thin liquid crystal layer with a swirl of optic axes, adds or removes one unit of twist depending on the polarisation. That is the step left or right. At the end a spatial light modulator untwists one chosen position back into a plain beam, so a photon counter can read it.

    Now the noise. Dephasing uses one more half wave plate, set at 0°, which flips the sign of the vertical part. At each step that plate is either in or out, and the share of photon collection time spent in each setting, 1 − η/2 without it and η/2 with it, sets the dephasing strength η. Amplitude damping uses the same trick with the loss optics, two plate settings for the two pieces of the damping, collected for equal times. So the noise is never random in the lab. It is a weighted average of clean runs.

    The measurements are all eight step walks at θ = π/4, with three loss strengths, γ = 0.4, 0.854 and 0.93. For dephasing they compare none with full (η = 1). For damping they use μ = 0.2, 0.6 and 1.0. In every run they track the centre of mass, the average position of the surviving photons. Its slope over the later steps is the drift velocity. A drift of 1 would mean one place right at every step, the fastest the light can go.

    The columns come from my own density matrix model of the paper’s equations, and its drift values match the paper’s Eq. 1 to 3 and fitted slopes. The bench is a cartoon, not to scale, and the real experiment reads one position at a time with a single detector.

    The loss plate sits in the vertical path and is turned to half of arcsin √(1 − γ). That works out to about 25.4° for γ = 0.4, 11.2° for γ = 0.854 and 7.7° for γ = 0.93. The q plate has a charge of q = 1/2, so it shifts the twist of the light by 2q = 1 unit, which is one position. Wave plates around it at 135°, 135° and 0° make the shift follow H and V rather than circular polarisation.

    In the maths each kind of noise is a pair of Kraus operators, two ways a step can go, applied to the density matrix that holds both the amounts of H and V and the phase between them. For dephasing the two ways are leave it alone and flip the sign of V. For damping they are shrink V by √(1 − μ) and turn V into H with weight √μ. In the experiment each way becomes its own plate setting, and the photon collection time stands in for its probability.

    My charts and the bench figure use the same recipe on a lattice a little wider than the walk, with loss, coin and shift at every step, the noise applied as these operator pairs, and the light rescaled to the survivors.

    Noise doesn’t kill it

    Start with dephasing. With the phase fully scrambled (η = 1) the walk becomes a biased coin toss, a classical random process. You might expect a wave effect to vanish. It does not. The light still drifts towards the same edge, and the drift stays above zero for any loss above 0. On a lattice with edges, the authors show, every mode of this scrambled walk is squeezed against the boundary. The skin effect survives all the way to fully incoherent light.

    Once the phase is gone, what is left is a drift plus a spread, like dye carried down a river while it smears out. In Talk 09 those two were the transport term and the smoothing viscosity term.

    Past 0.854, noise helps

    At γ = 0.4 dephasing slows the light a lot. The clean walk drifts at 0.71 positions per step and the scrambled one at only 0.25. At γ = 0.854 the two are almost exactly equal, about 0.75. At γ = 0.93 the order flips and the scrambled walk is faster, 0.87 against 0.78. The measured centres of mass over the eight steps follow these slopes for all three losses.

    Why? In the clean walk, the authors explain, interference adds its own push on top of the lopsided loss. At weak loss that push helps the drift, so dephasing hurts. At strong loss it works against the drift, so scrambling it away lets the loss take over. They stress that this is not universal. In other models decoherence destroys the skin effect, for example when it comes from transport that is the same in both directions.

      No sweet spot

      In many earlier studies of noise and transport, a little noise helps and a lot hurts, with a best amount in between. People call it the Goldilocks effect, and the far end usually fails because very strong noise freezes a quantum system in place (the quantum Zeno effect). Not here. For large losses the drift keeps rising with dephasing and peaks at full dephasing, η = 1. The paper shows this in its supplementary figures, and my model gives the same trend at γ = 0.93, from 0.78 with no dephasing to 0.82 at η = 0.5 and 0.87 at η = 1.

      Damping first kills it

      Amplitude damping is a different animal, because it drains vertical light into horizontal light. Put it before the leaky element at γ = 0.4 and the drift drops as the damping grows, with fitted slopes of 0.66 at μ = 0.2 and 0.50 at μ = 0.6. At full damping (μ = 1) the drift is zero and the light spreads into a plain bell curve centred on the start.

      The reason is neat. Full damping turns every photon horizontal at the start of each step. The leaky element only takes vertical light, so it has nothing to remove, and the coin splits each photon evenly between left and right. A fair coin toss again. Even at γ = 0.93, full damping before the loss leaves zero drift.

        Damping second keeps it

        Swap the order so the damping comes right after the leaky element, and the story changes. At γ = 0.4 the drift still falls as the damping grows, 0.68 at μ = 0.2 and 0.60 at μ = 0.6, but it levels off at 0.25 at full damping instead of reaching zero. At γ = 0.93 the damping speeds the light up, from 0.78 at μ = 0.2 and 0.79 at μ = 0.6 to 0.87 at full damping. The paper’s Eq. 3 gives the full damping value directly, and at θ = π/4 it works out to γ / (2 − γ), which is 0.25 and 0.87 for the two losses.

        Same optics, same noise, opposite result. With the damping second and the loss acts first, so its bias is already in the light when the reset happens. Dephasing, by contrast, gives the same result whichever side of the loss it sits on.

        Theory and experiment agree

        To compare whole distributions rather than slopes, the team scored the similarity between each measured distribution and its prediction, where 1 is a perfect match. For every setting it stayed above 0.9868 ± 0.0005 across all eight steps. The error bars come from photon counting statistics.

        04

        It matters? How?

        A flow that heads one way no matter where it starts is useful. Think of the skin effect as a one way street for waves, a bit like a diode or a ratchet built into a material. The paper lists where people hope to use it, moving quantum states in one direction, sensing, a form of machine learning called quantum reservoir computing, and programmable flow.

        Every real device is noisy, so the practical question is whether the one way street survives the real world. This paper’s answer is mostly yes, and sometimes the noise even helps. The bigger lesson for me is that the type of noise and where it enters matter as much as how much there is. That is a design rule you can use, since you often get some say over where losses and noise sit. The authors frame the work as a bridge between quantum and classical skin effects, since one dial takes the same walk smoothly from one to the other.

        The catches

        The walk is short and never touches an edge. It covers positions −8 to +8, so the experiment measures the drift through the middle. The pile up at an edge comes from theory and supplementary calculations. The early steps also bend away from a straight line, which the authors put down to a known self accelerating start.

        The drift only describes the photons that survive. Each distribution is rescaled to the photons still in the walk, and most are gone by the end. In my model of their walk at γ = 0.93, about 1.5 % of the photons survive all eight steps without noise and about 0.7 % with full dephasing.

        The noise is emulated. Mixing clean plate settings by collection time reproduces the average behaviour of a noisy channel, which is what the theory needs. It is not a real environment with its own memory and slow wandering.

        Every measurement uses one coin angle, θ = π/4, and one kind of walk. The authors say plainly that the noise boost is not universal and depends on the model and the kind of noise. It is also an Article in Press, so the final edited version may still change. (One small slip. The Funding section says the work received no specific funding, while the Acknowledgements list several grants.)

        05

        TLDR?

        Leaky steps, noisy coins, one way light...

        1. 1Single photons took an eight step quantum walk where every step throws away part of the vertical light, which makes the surviving light drift to one side. That drift is the skin effect.
        2. 2Scrambling the phase never removed the drift, and past a loss of 0.854 it made the drift faster, 0.87 against 0.78 positions per step at γ = 0.93.
        3. 3Amplitude damping placed before the loss reset every photon and, at full strength, killed the drift at any loss.
        4. 4The same damping placed after the loss kept the drift alive and at strong loss sped it up, so where the noise enters matters as much as how much there is.

        06

        Personal Take

        As a mechanical engineer I was trained to treat noise as the enemy. You isolate it, filter it, damp it out. What I like about this paper is that it treats noise as one more line on the design sheet, with a size you can choose and a place in the chain you can choose too. The order result feels familiar. Put a filter before or after a nonlinear part of a system and you get two different machines, even with the same parts.

        I would still hold the headline loosely. Eight steps is a short walk, the drift is measured on the few photons that survive, and the noise is a careful average of clean settings rather than a real environment. None of that makes the result wrong, and the authors are upfront about most of it. I would just want to see it on a longer lattice, or where the losses are not eating almost all of the signal, before calling noise a free boost.

        The picture I keep coming back to is the Brownian ratchet, the textbook toy where random kicks, a lopsided shape and a push away from equilibrium give steady motion in one direction. This walk feels like a cousin of it, made of light. A one way flow that does not mind being shaken, and sometimes runs faster when it is, is a nice idea to keep in mind.
