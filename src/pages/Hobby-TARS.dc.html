Building my own TARS

2025TARS-AI V2Personal project3D printingFan buildSimulation

TARS, the walking slab of a robot from Interstellar, printed and put together from the TARS-AI community’s open build kit. Then I taught a simulated copy seven new moves.

See the new moves ↓ TARS-AI on GitHub ↗

01 — The story

Why build a robot from a film?

TARS is the robot in Christopher Nolan’s Interstellar (2014), and what first pulled me in was the design. It is just four plain slabs, nothing added for show, yet those same blocks walk, roll, carry people and fold out into arms. Simple, and every part earns its place.

It also comes from before the AI boom. In 2014 a machine that talks back, cracks jokes and lets you turn its honesty and humour up or down was pure science fiction, and that speech and those small functions are what interest me most. Today they are within reach of a hobby build.

And somehow the box feels human. The middle slab with its screen reads like a torso and a face, the slabs beside it like arms and legs. Nothing about it tries to look human, yet the shape itself keeps reminding me of a person.

TARS-AI is an open, fan made recreation: printable parts, a Raspberry Pi as the brain, servos driven by a PCA9685 servo board, a screen for a face, and software that listens for a wake word, answers in a voice like TARS and plays its moves. Everything is shared on GitHub with a step by step build guide.

In 2025 I printed the parts for the V2 version and followed that build kit, piece by piece. Once it stood, I wanted to give it more personality than the moves it ships with, without risking a fall off the desk while I experimented. So the new moves start life in a simulation.

Credit where it’s due

I did not design TARS or this robot. I printed and assembled the TARS-AI community’s open design as a hobby, for non commercial use under its CC BY-NC 4.0 licence. This is a fan made project, not connected to the film or its studios.

Based on the mechanical puppet designs by Christopher Nolan, Nathan Crowley, and the production team who originally brought TARS to life. Miniaturized CAD by Charlie Diaz, with additional modifications by the TARS-AI Community.

TARS-AI includes AI-generated elements inspired by Bill Irwin’s portrayal of TARS in the film Interstellar.

What is mine: the printing and assembly, and the seven new moves and their tuning, made in my own simulation. The CAD and the robot’s software are unchanged.

02 — New moves

Teaching TARS seven new moves, in simulation first

Each clip loops on its own. Every move is written in the same servo steps the real robot uses, so it can be copied across once it has proved itself here.

How the simulation works

The simulated TARS is built part by part from the community’s V2 CAD in MuJoCo, the same version as mine, a physics engine: every printed part has its own shape, the legs ride on the sprung suspension, and the nine servo channels (height, two legs, and main arm, forearm and claw on each side) move at the real servos’ speed.

It replays the robot’s own moves exactly as its servo code runs them, one PWM tick at a time. The new moves use the same building blocks: set the legs, set the arms, wait.

Each move has one number to tune: how big it is. A bigger move reads better from across the room, but it can tip TARS over or shuffle it across the desk. So a script tries every size from 0.6 to 2 times the design, and keeps the biggest one that leaves TARS upright (under 15° of tilt), in place (under 15 mm of drift) and facing the same way (under 5° of turn). It stays one notch back from any size that falls over.
