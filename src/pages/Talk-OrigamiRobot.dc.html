Talk 11 · Review

Folded Twice, Moves Eight Ways. A Bistable Origami Robot

Rayang T Mendrofa · 30th October 2026 · 12 min read

Paper under reviewNATURE COMMUNICATIONS · ARTICLE IN PRESS · OPEN ACCESS

Bistable origami robot for adaptive multimodal motion actuated by nonuniform magnetic fields

Chong Huang, Zhuangzhi Miao, Sida Ma, Zheguo Tang, Heming Wang, Zhanpeng Cui, Yihao Fu and Yang Li · Wuhan University, with the Chinese Academy of Sciences’ State Key Laboratory of Transducer Technology

Nature Communications (2026), article in press: volume and pages not yet assigned
Received 21 January 2026 · Accepted 5 October 2026 · CC BY 4.0, code under the MIT License

doi:10.1038/s41467-026-79010-w ↗

Background: the robot working through the paper’s second obstacle track, crawling, rolling, tumbling, jumping and scampering. Image: adapted (cropped) from Huang et al., Nature Communications (2026), CC BY 4.0.

Most robots I know have one personality. This one has two, and it swaps between them when you slap it with a magnet. Open, it is a flat little sheet of origami that shuffles along the floor like a nervous inchworm. Closed, it is a capsule that rolls, jumps and hugs whatever it caught, without spending a single drop of power to keep hold. The whole thing weighs 0.81 g, less than a gram of plastic, film and four tiny magnets.

The clever part is the folding, not the magnets. Magnetic mini robots usually need a very precise, very even magnetic field, which means sitting inside a cage of coils where only a small middle region works well. This team from Wuhan University moves the smartness into the robot’s shape instead, so the coils can be simple, sit underneath an open platform, and produce a field that is deliberately uneven. If you read Talk 07 about the hair clip fish, you already know the trick at its heart, a structure with two comfortable resting shapes and an energy hill in between.

01

What’s in the paper?

The question is simple to say and hard to do. Can a tiny untethered robot both change shape and move as a whole, in lots of different ways, without needing an expensive, perfectly even magnetic field? The authors’ answer is a folded robot whose geometry is “pre programmed”, so the field only has to give it a push in roughly the right direction.

The robot starts from an origami gripper pattern: two mirror image Miura vertices (the classic folding pattern used for maps and solar panels) on either side of a central fold. Paper thin origami like this has a weakness, though. At certain points the fold can go two ways, and a robot with no motor on every hinge can wander down the wrong path. So the team switched to thick panels, which physically block the wrong path, and then did something sneaky with the hinges.

The robot

sub centimetre

Rigid panels of PLA plastic, 3D printed on a Bambu Lab H2D, joined by 0.1 mm silicone film (Young’s modulus 1.26 MPa) that acts as the hinges. Four N35 neodymium magnets, each 1.5 mm across and 3 mm long with a remanence of 1.2 T, are glued into the panels. Closed, it is about the size and shape of a capsule.

The sneaky hinges

hinge offsets

The hinges are deliberately shifted off the panel faces by small offsets. With the right offsets, the panels only fit together perfectly when fully open or fully closed. Anywhere in between they clash, so the silicone hinges have to stretch and bend, storing energy. That stored energy is the hill between two valleys, which makes the robot bistable.

    The team modelled the stored energy as two parts. The stretching energy of the silicone hinges rises and then falls as the clashing panels pass each other, while the bending energy keeps rising with the fold angle. Add them and you get a curve with a hump. It is open at an angle θ of 0, peaks near 0.6 rad and has a second valley near 1.45 rad. A finite element model in COMSOL backed up the hand calculation.

    Smaller offset ratios give a taller hill and a stronger snap, but also need a bigger magnetic kick. The team settled on offsets of 3.3 and 1.8 mm to balance the grip, the crawling stride, what they could build by hand and the most torque the magnets can give. Hand building has limits. Each crease was off by about 0.15 mm, enough to spoil the bistability, so they compensated by adjusting panel thickness. After 205 close and open cycles, the measured peak force showed the snap still worked.

    02

    So, how did they do it?

    Instead of chasing a uniform field, the team built a rig that is happy with an uneven one. Three coils and a small electromagnet sit under the robot’s platform on a carriage that slides on two perpendicular rails, so the coils can follow the robot around a large open area. The magnets in the robot feel two things from this uneven field: a twisting torque that folds the hinge, and a pulling force from the field’s gradient that moves the whole body.

    How hard and how fast the field pushes decides what happens. A small oscillating field nudges the robot without tipping it over the energy hill, so it wiggles and moves in whatever way its current shape allows. One large, short pulse throws it over the hill and it snaps into its other shape. Same robot, same coils, very different results.

      My own drawing, not to scale, after the paper’s Fig. 1G and Methods. It plays a short guided tour when it scrolls into view. Click any part to take over.

      The field from the coils is calculated with the Biot and Savart law, treating each coil as a continuous block of current plus the extra field from its iron core. For real time control that integral is chopped into a finite set of current filaments, which is much cheaper to compute. Field values from this model agreed with simulation along a test line.

      For the forces on the robot, each little magnet is treated as a pair of “magnetic charges” at its north and south ends. The force on the robot is the sum of charge times field at each end, and the folding torque is the sum of each force times its lever arm about the central hinge. Against finite element simulation and measurements, this charge model followed the trends with errors below 20% at distances beyond 2 mm. Magnets more than 5 mm apart barely affect each other, so the team ignored those internal forces.

      Same nudge, different dance

      Open, the robot is stiff but can still flex a little. The field pulls its rear magnets harder than its front ones, and the team gave the front and rear feet different friction, so each small fold and unfold turns into a shuffle forward. Turn the field at an angle to the hinge and the same trick makes it turn. Closed, the same kind of small oscillating field makes the side panels flutter so it scampers, or, with all three coils driven together, makes the whole capsule roll. Rolling was its fastest move, at 5.45 body lengths per second at 2 Hz, with half a cycle of the field rolling it half way round.

        One pulse, a closed fist

        Fire one pulse from the small electromagnet and the robot snaps shut. The paper’s photos show it going from open to closed in about 0.06 s, with the tips of its panels reaching several hundred millimetres per second. Fire a pulse the other way and it pops open again. In between, the energy hill does the holding: once closed, it stays closed with the coils switched off, which the authors call self locking.

        That grip is surprisingly firm for something under a gram. Holding a small sphere, it resisted a drag of 26.5 mN before the sphere slipped. It kept hold of spheres, cubes and tetrahedrons when dropped from a high platform onto the floor. The heaviest load it could hold depended on the object’s size, peaking with a 6 mm sphere at roughly 2.7 g, more than three times its own weight, because at that size its fold sits closest to the point of peak force.

        The demo everyone will remember is a live silkworm, caught as it crawled, carried and released unharmed. The number nobody should forget is next to it. Out of 100 tries, the capture worked 4 times. The authors are honest that this is a proof of concept, not a reliable trap.

        To work out when a pulse is enough, the team treated the snap as slow and steady (quasi static) and compared the magnetic torque on the central hinge with the elastic torque resisting it along the whole fold. If the magnetic curve stays above the elastic one, the robot goes all the way over. This ignores the bang of the panels hitting the object, so it predicts when it will close, not how hard.

        Obstacle courses, slowly

        The finale puts it all together on two tracks. In the first, the robot crawls to a channel too narrow for it, closes and rolls through, meets a beam too low to roll under, opens and crawls beneath it, tumbles over a low wall, then grips a cargo, carries it and lets it go. In the second, it pulls a cargo stuck to the floor free with a sideways shove, jumps a high wall while still holding it, and switches to scampering to get through a low tunnel in a tank of water.

        It is a great demo, and the numbers underneath it deserve a look. Single modes mostly worked more than 90% of the time. Switching between modes was shakier, with some switches succeeding less than 35% of the time. A full course in one uninterrupted run succeeded only about 7.1% of the time. Much of the time went on a person watching and nudging the coil carriage into position by eye. From the time stamps in the paper’s figure, the first course took about 12 minutes and the second about 15.

        04

        It matters? How?

        Most magnetic mini robots are built around the field, with a carefully tuned cage of coils and a small sweet spot in the middle. That is fine in a lab and a problem in a hospital, where the patient has to fit inside the cage. This paper flips the burden. The robot’s folds decide how it responds, so the field can be uneven, the coils can sit underneath an open platform, and the hardware stays fairly cheap, with off the shelf power supplies, amplifiers and signal generators.

        The authors point at capsule endoscopy. Closed, the robot is about capsule shaped, and its 26.5 mN grip is more than their estimate of the drag from gut contractions on something this small, and comparable with the low end of reported capsule resistance in the intestine. They call that “preliminary potential”, and so should we. For small grippers, it joins a strange family. In Talk 03 the gripper was a dead spider, and here it is a folded sheet that holds on with no power at all. And like the walking modules in Talk 06, it gets many kinds of movement out of very simple parts.

        The catches

        Capture of a moving target worked 4 times in 100. Mode switches sometimes dropped below 35% success, and a full course in one run managed about 7.1%. The mechanical tests use 5 repeats, mostly on a single prototype, and the robots were assembled by hand.

        A person watched the robot and moved the coil carriage by eye. There is no closed loop control or imaging yet, and the gantry itself is only accurate to about ±2 mm sideways and ±1.5 mm vertically. “Low uniformity” fields still need a moving rig and an operator.

        The radar chart comparing it with earlier robots plots unreported values at a baseline and some values are estimated, so it is a sketch, not a ranking. Tests were on dry surfaces and simple water, not slippery, curved gut. PLA and silicone are not clinical materials. It is an article in press: peer reviewed and accepted, but the final edited version is not out yet.

        05

        TLDR?

        How to get eight moves out of one fold...

        1. 1Offset the hinges of a thick panel origami robot so its panels only fit when fully open or fully closed, so it now has two stable shapes.
        2. 2Small oscillating magnetic fields wiggle it without flipping it, so it crawls or turns when open and scampers or rolls when closed.
        3. 3One big pulse snaps it over the energy hill in about 0.06 s to grab something, and it stays shut with the power off.
        4. 4Because the shape does the clever part, simple coils under an open platform can drive it, though full obstacle runs still succeed only rarely.

        06

        Personal Take

        What I like most here is the phrase “structural intelligence”. As a mechanical engineer, I find it satisfying when the cleverness lives in the geometry rather than in a pile of electronics. A few millimetres of hinge offset turn a floppy sheet into something with two opinions about how it should sit, and that one design decision is what lets the coils be simple. It is the same lesson as the hair clip fish: get the shape right, and the actuator can be lazy.

        I also appreciate that the paper prints its awkward numbers. A 4% capture rate and a 7.1% clean run rate are not the kind of figures most teams put next to their best video. They make the result more believable, not less. My bigger doubt is with the word “large workspace”: the field can be uneven, but the coils still have to be driven around underneath by a gantry and a human with good eyesight. Take away the operator and add real feedback, and then I will call it simple.

        If I had a desk and a weekend, I would want to see how far the trick scales. Could the same offset rule be printed in one go, without hand assembly and its 0.15 mm errors? Could the energy hill be tuned so the closed state grips harder without needing a bigger pulse? And could the camera that the operator replaced be put back in the loop? Fold that into the next paper and this little robot stops being a demo and starts being a tool.
