Talk 12 — Review

Practice Makes Plastic. Brain Cells After One Typing Lesson

Rayang T Mendrofa · 6th November 2026 · 14 min read

Paper under reviewPLOS BIOLOGY · SHORT REPORT · OPEN ACCESS

Learning engages transient and sustained cellular mechanisms in the human brain

Guillermina Griffa, Marco Palombo, Abraham Yeffal, Hong-Hsi Lee, Agustin Solano, Susie Y. Huang and Valeria Della-Maggiore · University of Buenos Aires, Cardiff University, University of San Martín, Massachusetts General Hospital and Harvard Medical School, McGill University

PLOS Biology 24(6): e3003861 (2026)
Received 12 December 2025 · Accepted 4 June 2026 · Published 18 June 2026

doi:10.1371/journal.pbio.3003861 ↗

Background: brain maps from the paper’s Fig 2B, the areas more active while people typed the sequence. Image: adapted from Griffa et al., PLOS Biology (2026), CC BY 4.0 (background darkened).

Every time you learn something, your brain is supposed to rebuild a little bit of itself. New connections sprout, old ones get trimmed, support cells wrap around the new joints to hold them in place. That is the textbook story, and almost all of the evidence for it comes from animals, usually mice, because the honest way to check is to look at brain tissue under a microscope. Volunteers tend to object to that part.

This paper tries to watch the rebuilding happen in living people, with no needles and no microscope. The tool is an MRI scanner built for unusually strong magnetic gradients, plus a model that splits what it measures into three kinds of space: inside cell bodies, inside thin cell branches, and the gaps between them. Twenty nine people learned a short finger sequence on a keyboard, and the team scanned them before, 30 minutes after and 24 hours after. The result is two different responses with two different timetables: one that fades within a day, and one that is still there the next morning. For an engineer, it is also a lovely example of getting information from a sensor at a scale far below its own resolution.

01

What’s in the paper?

The question is simple to say and hard to answer: when a brain learns, which changes are real rebuilding and which are just the tissue reacting to being busy? Hard working brain tissue is known to swell slightly for a while, as cells take up ions and water follows them in. That swelling is temporary housekeeping, not learning. Rebuilding, on the other hand, means more and longer cell branches: dendrites growing new contact points, and the arms of support cells called astrocytes reaching out to stabilise them.

The trouble is that the standard MRI measure of tissue microstructure, called mean diffusivity or MD, cannot tell those two apart. The same team showed in an earlier study that MD drops in some brain regions within half an hour of learning. But a drop in MD only says that water in that patch of brain has become a bit less free to move. Swollen cell bodies would do that. So would more branches. So would both.

So this paper runs the same learning experiment again, on a much stronger scanner, and adds a model called SANDI (soma and neurite density imaging) that tries to say which part of the tissue changed. The subjects are 29 healthy volunteers, 16 of them female, with no neurological or sleep problems. What changes between scans is time: before learning, 30 minutes after, and 24 hours after.

    The volunteers did 12 sequences per block (60 key presses), then rested for 25 seconds, for 15 blocks.

    “Per tap” is the intertap interval: the average time between taps within a correct sequence, the paper’s measure of speed. In its Fig 2A the group starts near 0.5 s per tap in block 1 and ends near 0.3 s by block 15 (read off the chart).

    People first memorised the sequence, then typed it at their own pace while lying in the scanner, which recorded brain activity (functional MRI) during the 15 blocks. The whole session took about 15 to 20 minutes. Structural scans came before training, 30 minutes after and 24 hours after. After the 24 hour scan, everyone did 8 more blocks to check how much they had kept overnight.

    02

    So, how did they do it?

    Diffusion MRI does not photograph cells. It times how far water molecules wander, and works out the shape of the walls they keep bumping into.

    Here is the idea in engineering terms. Water molecules in tissue jiggle about randomly. In open water they spread out freely. Inside a cell body, which the model treats as a tiny sphere, they hit the membrane and stay put. Inside a thin branch, treated as a stick, they can travel along it but not across it. The scanner tags each molecule’s position with a magnetic gradient pulse, waits, and then checks how far it has drifted. In this study the pulses lasted 8 ms and the wait (the diffusion time) was 24 ms.

    How hard the scanner “leans” on that motion is called the b value. A low b value only notices large, easy movements; a high b value is sensitive to the tiny, restricted ones trapped inside small cells. Strong gradients are what let you reach high b values while keeping the timing short, and the original 3T Connectome scanner used here was built for exactly that, with gradients of up to 300 mT/m. The team collected seven b values from 200 to 8,000 s/mm², with 32 or 64 gradient directions each, plus 22 reference images with no gradient at all.

    Then comes the trick. Every 2 mm voxel holds a huge number of cells, so no single cell is ever seen. But the way the signal fades as the b value rises has a different shape for water in spheres, water in sticks and free water. Fit the measured curve with a mix of those three shapes and you get three fractions that add up to one: soma (cell bodies), neurite (cell branches, including the branches of support cells) and extracellular (the space between). That is what the paper means by sub voxel sensitivity: a 2 mm sensor reporting on structures far smaller than itself, through physics rather than resolution.

    Values are group means read off the paper’s Fig 3, so the third decimal is approximate. The water walk is a toy simulation, slowed down, and the cells are a cartoon whose changes are magnified ten times; dial scales are my choices. The real scan covers the whole head, not one cube.

    The analysis went in two steps. First, the standard method (a diffusion tensor fit, giving MD) found the brain regions where MD changed after learning, using a statistics test across the whole brain. Then the team read SANDI’s three fractions inside exactly those regions and followed them over the three scans. Because picking regions with one method and testing them with a related one can flatter the result, they also ran a second check that does not depend on the first: they divided each person’s hippocampus into head, body and tail from an ordinary anatomical scan, and fitted both models there independently.

    Diffusion scans: a spin echo sequence with 2 × 2 × 2 mm voxels, repetition time 4,200 ms, echo time 55 ms, diffusion time 24 ms, gradient pulse 8 ms. b values of 200, 500, 1,200 and 2,400 s/mm² with 32 directions each; 4,000, 6,000 and 8,000 s/mm² with 64 directions each; and 22 images at b = 0, five of them with the opposite phase direction to correct distortion. The scanner’s gradients reach 300 mT/m with a slew rate of 200 T/m/s, read out through a 64 channel head coil.

    SANDI models cell bodies as spheres, cell branches as sticks and the rest as freely spreading water, and assumes water does not swap between them during the measurement. The extracellular fraction is simply what is left: soma + neurite + extracellular = 1. MD was computed from the b = 1,200 s/mm² images alone.

    Learning in the breaks

    Before trusting anything new, the team checked that the new scanner reproduced their earlier study. It did. Performance improved over training, and the gains came during the 25 second rests between blocks (micro offline gains, p = 0.034), not during typing itself (p = 0.59). Brain activity during the rests rose in the left hippocampus and the precuneus, while the usual movement circuits did the work during typing.

    Four regions tighten up

    Thirty minutes after learning, MD dropped in four places: the back of the left hippocampus (memory), the right precuneus (on the inner face of the parietal lobe), the left primary motor cortex or M1 (the brain’s main movement area), and the left posterior parietal cortex or PPC. The paper calls M1 and PPC central to learning motor skills. In the hippocampus and M1 the drop was gone by 24 hours. In the precuneus and PPC it was still there.

      A swelling that fades

      The soma fraction went up at 30 minutes in all four regions, then returned to baseline at 24 hours. Half an hour is far too short to grow new cells, so the authors read this as cell bodies briefly swelling. Their proposed mechanism is ordinary physiology: busy neurons shift ions into the space around them, neighbouring cells mop those ions up, and water follows by osmosis. The fact that it happened the same way in four very different regions, all of which were active during the task, fits a general housekeeping response rather than anything specific to learning. The soma changes also had no link to how well anyone learned.

      Branches that stay

      The neurite fraction told a different story. In the precuneus and PPC it rose by 30 minutes and was still raised the next day, which is what you would expect if dendrites and astrocyte branches were remodelling. And this one did track behaviour: people with larger micro offline gains showed bigger neurite changes (F(1,25) = 6.15, p = 0.020), and the link was stronger at 24 hours than at 30 minutes (interaction p = 0.05). Neither the hippocampus nor M1 showed a lasting neurite change. The paper’s abstract calls the two lasting regions “key motor regions”; to be precise, they are the two parietal regions, not the motor cortex itself.

      The independent check agrees

      In the hippocampus, split into head, body and tail without using any diffusion data, MD fell and the soma fraction rose together at 30 minutes in the body and tail of the left hippocampus, and both recovered by 24 hours. Nothing changed in the head or on the right side, and the neurite fraction did not move anywhere in the hippocampus. Same pattern as the main analysis, found a different way.

      04

      It matters? How?

      For about twenty years, MRI studies of learning in people have reported changes in one lumped number, and argued about what it meant. This paper shows that the same drop in MD can hide two different processes with different timetables: a short lived swelling that probably means “this tissue was busy”, and a slower, lasting change in cell branches that looks much more like actual rebuilding. Being able to tell them apart, without opening anyone’s head, is the step that lets human studies talk to the decades of animal work. The authors also see uses beyond learning: telling healthy remodelling from inflammation or shrinkage in disease, for example.

      The timing is interesting too. The lasting changes in the parietal cortex were already visible 30 minutes after practice, before any sleep. In Talk 05 I covered people solving puzzles inside their dreams, and sleep is usually given a big role in fixing memories. This paper does not say sleep is unimportant (its design cannot test that), but it suggests the cortex starts rebuilding while you are still awake. And compared with Talk 08, where every neuron of a fly was traced from electron microscope slices of a dead brain, this is the opposite trade: far coarser, but in a living human, over time. No implants either, unlike the chips of Talk 10; just water, magnets and a clever model.

      The catches

      No control group in this study. Everyone learned the task. The claim that the changes come from learning, not just from moving fingers or lying in a scanner, leans on the team’s earlier study, which did have a control group doing plain tapping, and on this study reproducing its results. That is reasonable, but it is not the same as testing it again.

      A model, not a microscope. The fractions are the model’s best fit, not counted cells. They must add up to one, so if one rises another has to fall. The model ignores water swapping between compartments, and at a 24 ms diffusion time it underestimates neurite density compared with histology, so it sees thick branches, not synapses. The authors say all of this themselves, and note that comparing each region with its own baseline softens the problem. (Small slip: the Methods give the diffusion time as 24 ms, the Discussion as 23 ms.)

      Size and statistics. 29 young, healthy, right handed adults and one task. Several p values sit close to 0.05 (0.044, 0.043, and an interaction at p = 0.05), and the main SANDI analysis was run inside regions chosen with MD, which the hippocampus check only partly addresses. One person was left out of the brain activity analysis and one of the hippocampus statistics because of a technical problem and missing data. It is a short report; I would want to see it repeated on another scanner and another task.

      05

      TLDR?

      One voxel, three compartments, two timetables...

      1. 1Twenty nine people learned a five tap finger sequence and were scanned before, 30 minutes after and 24 hours after.
      2. 2A very strong gradient scanner and the SANDI model split each 2 mm voxel’s signal into cell bodies, cell branches and the space between.
      3. 3Cell bodies swelled briefly in all four active regions and were back to normal the next day, which looks like housekeeping.
      4. 4Cell branches got denser in two parietal regions and stayed that way overnight, which looks like real rebuilding, and it tracked how well people learned.

      06

      Personal Take

      What hooked me is that this is really an instrumentation paper wearing a neuroscience coat. A sensor with 2 mm pixels is used to say something about structures a few micrometres across, not by zooming in but by modelling how a tracer (water) moves through the material. Mechanical engineers do the same kind of thing all the time, inferring cracks from vibration or porosity from flow, and it always comes with the same warning label: your answer is only as good as your model.

      That is also why I would hold the conclusions a little loosely. The spheres, sticks and free water picture is a useful simplification, the three fractions are forced to add to one, and the effects are small: a shift of about 0.01 in a fraction near 0.2 to 0.4. I like that the authors list these limits clearly, and that they checked the hippocampus a second way. What I would really want is a control group in the same scanner, and the same experiment run in an animal where you can scan first and then look under a microscope.

      Still, the headline idea sticks with me. Fifteen to twenty minutes of practice, and part of the brain is measurably different the next day, while another part just needed a short cool down. It makes a decent argument for taking the breaks between practice seriously, since that is where these volunteers actually got faster.
