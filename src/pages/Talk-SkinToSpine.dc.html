Talk 07 — Double review

“Skin to Spine: When a Sticky Electrode Meets an Artificial Neuron”

When you decide to walk, your brain doesn’t send a separate order for every step. It nudges a circuit in your spinal cord, a central pattern generator, that already knows how to make a rhythm: left, right, bend, straighten. Meanwhile your skin is the boundary where the outside world, and anyone trying to listen in on your body, has to make contact.

This month I’m reviewing two papers together, because each one rebuilds one of those two parts. The first is a soft, sticky material that can listen to the body through the skin without any gel. The second is a tiny artificial neuron that makes walking rhythms for a robot dog using about as much energy per spike as a real neuron. Neither paper mentions the other. Put side by side, though, they look like two ends of the same wire.

01 · Paper A

An electrode that doesn’t need gel

Most heart tracings in a hospital are recorded through “wet” electrodes: a silver/silver-chloride snap with a blob of conductive gel and a ring of aggressive tape. They work very well for a short time. Over hours the gel dries out, it can irritate skin, and the tape hurts coming off. That is a problem if you want to wear a sensor for days.

The team from NC State started from POMaC, a soft, rubbery polyester made from citric acid that is already used in tissue engineering. It is gently sticky and bends like skin, but it doesn’t conduct. So they mixed in PEDOT:PSS, the standard soft conducting polymer. That alone didn’t work.

The final recipe uses 6 wt.% PEDOT:PSS pellets and 4 v/v% DBSA in 10 mL of POMaC prepolymer, mixed in an ethanol-rich ethanol/water solvent. A pure-ethanol version kept turning solid in the mixer, so a little water was added. Without DBSA, the cured film is so dry that the PEDOT chains never connect; the four-point probe read it as an open circuit even after steam treatments. With DBSA, the film reaches about 50 S/cm (≈ 0.02 Ω·cm) straight after curing. The mix is spread through a stencil onto polyimide at about 45°, a simple screen-printing trick, so electrodes can be patterned to any shape.

Water is the obvious worry for something meant to sit on skin. Soaked in water, σPOMaC’s resistivity goes up as the film swells and the conducting paths get pulled apart. The change is temporary: once it dries (64 hours later in the test), the resistance falls back. Putting it on skin and peeling it off again several times didn’t change its resistance either.

Paper A, Fig. 2. (a) Resistivity after different wetting treatments; the DBSA-free version was off the scale every time. (b, c) Impedance from 1 mHz to 100 kHz. (d) A 5 Hz square wave passing through the film in saline.

Source: Queener et al., Adv. Electron. Mater. 12, e00004 (2026), CC BY 4.0. Figure reproduced unaltered.

From “nothing” to 50 S/cm, straight out of the oven.

Then comes the part that makes it an electrode rather than a conductor: it sticks by itself. On PDMS it peels at 0.013 N/mm, and on steel at 0.040 N/mm. Medical tapes, including the foam ring on a standard 3M Red Dot electrode, sit at 1.6 to 12 N/mm. That is the point, though. σPOMaC is sticky enough to stay put and soft enough to follow every wrinkle, but it comes off with no residue. It even stuck to a finger under water.

0.013 N/mm, under water

Sticky enough to stay on a wet finger, gentle enough to peel off with no residue.

Each sample was peeled repeatedly. The 3M electrodes left adhesive along their edges; σPOMaC came off clean. Interestingly, the σPOMaC samples on steel got tackier when re-tested five days later, so a fourth peel was added. The base elastomer held a sustained shear load of about 11.7 kPa for up to four hours before the test rig, not the bond, slipped.

Source: Queener et al., Adv. Electron. Mater. 12, e00004 (2026), CC BY 4.0. Figure reproduced unaltered.

The real test was a heartbeat. Three volunteers wore a custom chest patch with σPOMaC electrodes alongside standard 3M gel electrodes, first for five minutes at rest and then for an hour of sitting, standing and pacing. The traces line up beat for beat. The numbers tell the more honest story.

The electrodes were 9 mm discs. The wireless circuit board was held on with silicone tape around the patch edge, never over the electrode itself; one subject accidentally wore it without the tape and the resting trace was still clean. On the bench, σPOMaC’s impedance was 735–1000 kΩ at 1 Hz, falling to 6.5–10 kΩ at 1 kHz. 3M gel electrodes are around 10–100 kΩ and 1–10 kΩ. So the material is still much more resistive at the slow frequencies where heart signals live, which matches the lower signal-to-noise. Skin-to-electrode impedance wasn’t measured directly.

02 · Paper B

A neuron that remembers it’s switched on

In your spinal cord there are neurons with a useful habit. Give them a short push and they don’t just fire once; they jump to a raised “plateau” and keep firing until something pushes them back down. Two groups of these, wired to inhibit each other, are a natural metronome: one group drives the muscles that straighten a leg, the other the muscles that bend it, never both at once.

The team from Zhejiang University built that habit into hardware. Their artificial plateau neuron has two parts. The first is a plateau gate: a transistor made from an atom-thin layer of molybdenum disulfide (MoS₂) with a graphene “floating gate” buried underneath that can trap charge, so it remembers its last setting without power. The second is a threshold switch, a sandwich of silver, aluminium oxide and gold, that fires a spike every time enough current builds up. Feed in a steady train of small 0.6 V pulses, and the gate decides whether those pulses become spikes.

The floating-gate stack is MoS₂/Al₂O₃/graphene/Al₂O₃. A 10 ms pulse of −10 V on the plateau gate (VPG) puts the neuron into its active state, where it integrates the 0.6 V input pulses and fires; a 100 ms pulse of +10 V shuts it down completely. Because the stored charge leaks away slowly, an active neuron’s spikes fade gradually over time, a bit like a real plateau winding down. Changing how hard the gate is set changes both how tall the spikes are and how often they fire, which is the “spike-malleability” in the title.

Pair two of these on one input and you have a flexor–extensor oscillator, the 2(PG-TS) circuit. Toggle their gates in turn and the bursts come out exactly 180° apart, like the elbow muscles in the paper’s first demo. Put four plateau gates in parallel (4PG-TS) and each one acts as a stored weight, so the neuron adds up four weighted inputs before it fires: a multiply-and-add done by physics instead of code. They made them on a 2-inch wafer and checked spikes from 20 separate circuits for consistency.

The energy is where it gets striking. At 500 Hz the circuit draws about 141 nanowatts, which works out to 141.37 picojoules per spike, with a response delay of 5.62 microseconds. The paper compares that against other hardware rhythm generators.

The 2(PG-TS) has three modes: extensor only, flexor only, and both in alternation. Across 1,200 spikes the extensor and flexor currents were essentially uncorrelated, which is what you want for clean, independent muscle commands. The comparison in Table 1 also lists transistor count: 2 here, against 1,296 for a CMOS Matsuoka oscillator and 76 for a pulse-type CMOS design. Frequency range is 0–500 Hz, matching the range quoted for biological neurons.

Source: Wang et al., Nat. Commun. 17, 5801 (2026), CC BY 4.0. Figure reproduced unaltered.

Finally, the robot. A Unitree Go2 has 12 motors; the team locked the four hips and drove the other eight (thigh and calf on each leg). Four flexor–extensor pairs were enough, because the legs move in diagonal pairs: front-left with back-right, front-right with back-left. Each pair’s extensor spikes minus its flexor spikes, smoothed with a Gaussian filter, becomes a joint angle that the robot’s ordinary position controllers follow. The result is a steady trot at 0.15 m/s, first in simulation and then on the floor.

Turning the input up (with a little help from light shining on the devices) gave three gaits on command: stepping on the spot, a cautious walk at 0.15 m/s and an energetic walk at 0.4 m/s, keeping a 200 ms offset between left and right. At 0.4 m/s the whole robot used 13.64% less motor power than with its usual microcontroller-based controller.

03

How the two papers fit together

On the surface these are very different papers: one is materials chemistry and medical sensing, the other is 2D-material electronics and robotics. What they share is a philosophy. Both take a part that is normally rigid, power-hungry or uncomfortable, and replace it with something that behaves more like the body: soft and sticky in one case, sparse and spiky in the other.

The more interesting link is direction. Paper A is a way in: it lets a machine hear the body. Paper B is a way out: it turns a simple command into coordinated movement without a processor working out every joint angle. Chain them, and you get a loop that looks a lot like the nervous system in my first paragraph: skin to spine to step. Neither paper builds that chain, so it’s worth walking through which links already exist.

05

The experiment worth doing

There is a real piece of research hiding between these two papers. It could show that this particular electrode material improves comfort, repeatability or signal stability compared with gel, or that pairing it with a rhythm controller gives a measurable advantage over the usual setup.

The weak point in the “skin to spine” story is an assumption: that recording an electrical signal makes it directly usable for control. It doesn’t, automatically. A recording only has to be readable afterwards. A control signal has to be right on every step, arrive on time, and mean the same thing tomorrow as it did today. So the strongest next step isn’t a better electrode or a better neuron. It is investigating the interface between them.

Research question

Can self-adhesive conductive elastomer electrodes record muscle activity reliably enough to modulate a robot’s walking rhythm, and how does their performance compare with standard gel electrodes?

How you could test it

  1. 1Put σPOMaC and gel electrodes side by side on the same leg muscles, such as the shin and calf, and record while people walk on a treadmill at several speeds. Compare signal-to-noise, motion artefacts and baseline drift.
  2. 2Take them off and put them back on over several days. Record how much the signal changes between sessions, and ask participants how comfortable they were and check the skin afterwards.
  3. 3Turn muscle activity into a few simple features (when each burst starts, the step rate, how hard the muscle works) and score how often each electrode gets them right against a foot switch or motion sensor.
  4. 4Let those features set the gates of a plateau-neuron oscillator, or a software model of one, and measure how closely the robot’s steps follow the person: timing error, lag, and missed or extra steps.
  5. 5Compare gel and σPOMaC through the whole chain. The new electrode only “wins” if the robot follows at least as well, not just if the raw recording looks clean.

Could it help a robot mimic the user?

Partly. Muscle signals (EMG) tell you when and how hard muscles fire, not where the limbs are. That makes skin electrodes good at timing and intent: start, stop, step rate, which phase of the stride you are in, how much effort you are putting in. Those happen to be exactly the dials a plateau-neuron rhythm exposes: on or off, burst strength and frequency. Muscle activity also begins a few tens of milliseconds before the limb actually moves, so a well-decoded signal could let a robot respond slightly earlier than a camera watching the same leg.

Copying the exact shape of a movement, though, such as joint angles and where each foot lands, needs kinematics from motion sensors or cameras. And a better electrode only improves pattern accuracy if it cuts noise during movement, which is precisely where σPOMaC currently trails gel (16 vs 29 dB). So “better electrodes, better mimicry” is a hypothesis to test, not something either paper shows. Where σPOMaC could genuinely help is long sessions: no gel drying out, gentle re-application, and printed electrode layouts that land in the same place every day.

One person, many robots?

In principle, yes, and it plays to Paper B’s strengths. Because each robot generates its own rhythm locally, the person only needs to send a few numbers per step (start, stop, speed, phase) rather than a constant stream of joint angles. Every robot runs its own oscillators and stays in step, much like the separate circuits on each leg of the Go2. Compared with computer vision or motion tracking, that needs no cameras and no line of sight, works in the dark or behind a wall, and uses very little bandwidth.

There are catches. Robots on different ground can’t safely copy the same step blindly: one on a slope needs a different foot placement from one on a flat floor, so each still needs its own sensing and reflexes. Muscle signals drift with fatigue, sweat and electrode position, so calibration matters. And it carries rhythm and intent, not full posture. A realistic version is a hybrid: skin electrodes for timing and intent, a couple of motion sensors for posture, and local sensing on every robot for balance.

06

It matters? How?

Wearable health sensors are only useful if people actually keep them on. An electrode that needs no gel, doesn’t hurt to remove, can be printed in any shape, and is made from a polymer already used inside the body is a practical step toward monitoring that lasts days instead of hours.

Robots, meanwhile, are hitting an energy wall. Every joint on a legged robot is normally steered by a processor running control loops thousands of times a second. Paper B shows that the repetitive part of walking, the rhythm, can be handed to a couple of transistors that use picojoules, leaving the processor free for decisions. That idea extends well beyond robot dogs: prosthetic legs, exoskeletons and rehabilitation devices all need steady, adjustable rhythms with a tiny battery.

And together, they point toward body-to-machine links that are soft at the skin and efficient at the core, rather than rigid sensors wired into a power-hungry computer.

The catches

Paper A: three participants, all women aged 20–30, one hour each, and only heart signals. While moving, signal quality (16 dB) fell below the level usually needed to read the full ECG shape, though beats were still clear. It is far more resistive than gel at low frequencies. The DBSA that makes it conduct is a strong acid: in unbuffered saline the extract dropped from pH 5.5 to 1.7, though buffered, body-like fluids changed much less.

Paper B: the neuron makes rhythm but doesn’t sense anything; the robot doesn’t feel the ground or correct a stumble. The hips were locked, the gait was a fixed diagonal trot, and switching needs ±10 V gate pulses. Smoothing spikes into joint angles and running the motors still happens in conventional electronics, so the 141 pJ/spike is the chip, while the system saving is the more modest 13.64%.

07

TLDR?

Two halves of a nervous system, built separately...

  1. 1Paper A mixes a soft citrate rubber with a conducting polymer and a surfactant to make an electrode that sticks to skin and needs no gel.
  2. 2It records heartbeats as well as gel electrodes at rest, a bit worse while walking, and peels off clean.
  3. 3Paper B builds a two-part artificial neuron that stays on or off after a short gate pulse, and pairs them into a flexor–extensor rhythm.
  4. 4That rhythm walks a robot dog at about the energy cost of real neurons per spike, and saves 13.64% of motor power.
  5. 5The real open question is the interface: can a sticky patch’s muscle signal steer the rhythm reliably, and does it beat gel from end to end?
  6. 6Join them and you have skin to spine. The missing piece is the middle: turning a millivolt body signal into a clean command for the neuron.

08

Personal Take

What I like about reading these two together is that neither tries to be the whole system. Biology splits the job too: your skin doesn’t decide when you walk, and your spinal cord doesn’t feel the floor directly. Each paper does one part well and is upfront about where it stops.

For Paper A, the headline numbers aren’t the ones I care about most. 50 S/cm is great, but the number that decides whether σPOMaC can replace gel is the 16 dB while moving. At rest it already does the job. Making it hold up under motion, and showing it on muscle and brain signals, which are smaller and noisier than a heartbeat, is what would turn a nice material into a standard part.

For Paper B, I’m impressed by how much rhythm comes out of two transistors. But I read the 141 pJ as a chip number. The robot still needs filtering, controllers and motor drivers, and the 13.64% saving is the more honest system figure. I’d love to see that gap close, and I’d love to see the rhythm react to something, because a pattern generator that can’t feel a stumble is only half of a spinal cord.

The experiment in section 05 is the one I would most like to see run. Not because it would make a flashy demo, but because it answers the question both papers quietly skip: is a good recording also a good command? If a sticky patch can switch a spine on and off, reliably, for days, the one-person-many-robots idea stops being a thought experiment.
