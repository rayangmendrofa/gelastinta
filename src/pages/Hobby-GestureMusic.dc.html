Gesture Music: an instrument you play with hand signs

2025 – 2026Personal projectComputer visionPython · OpenCV · MediaPipe

A webcam watches both of my hands. The shape each hand makes picks a note, a chord or a drum, how fast I make it sets how loud it plays, and moving the hand bends the sound. Everything can be looped, saved as MIDI or scored in a rhythm game.

01 — The story

A webcam that listens to your hands

Gesture Music turns an ordinary webcam into a musical instrument. Hold up one finger and it plays a note; hold up two and it plays the next one. A closed fist is silence. With the extended set, each hand has eight signs, a full octave on one hand.

Over time it grew into a small music studio: chords and drums for two hands, a looper with overdubs, live MIDI into music software, a rhythm game that writes its charts from your own songs, and a calibration wizard that learns how your fingers bend.

I also wrote a 61-page handbook that explains how to play it, how the code is built and the music and maths behind every part. It is at the end of this page.

github.com/rayangmendrofa/OpenCV-handsign-midi ↗

Credit where it’s due

by

The hand tracking is Google’s MediaPipe: its Hand Landmarker finds 21 points on each hand in every frame, and its Selfie Segmenter separates me from the room. Everything after that (reading the signs, the sound, the looper, the game) is my own code, released under the MIT licence.

02 — Timeline

From four fingers to a small studio

2025 – 2026

  1. 2025

    The start

    Four fingers, four notes

    MediaPipe gives 21 points on each hand. From the angles of each finger the code works out which fingers are up, and 1 to 4 raised fingers pick four notes. A fist means silence. This basic set is still the heart of the instrument.

  2. Then

    Two hands, one band

    Notes, chords and drums

    Modes for Notes, Chords, Drums and Band, so the left hand can drum while the right plays chords. A real-time synthesizer with 10 instruments and a 12-piece drum kit, the extended set of 8 signs, repeats by tapping or pumping, and hand height for volume or pitch bend.

  3. Then

    Record and share

    Looper, MIDI and demos

    A looper that records actions rather than audio, with overdub layers; live MIDI into music software; demo videos with the exact sound. The background blur went from 35 ms per frame in its first version to about 1 ms, by running alongside hand tracking.

  4. Then

    Play along

    A rhythm game

    Song sheets scroll toward a hit line, and you make the right sign on the beat: Perfect, Good or Miss, combos, grades and best scores.

  5. 11 Oct 2026

    On GitHub

    The repository opens

    The project gets its public home: rayangmendrofa/OpenCV-handsign-midi.

  6. 3 Nov 2026

    Released, with a handbook

    The 3 November edition

    The code goes up under the MIT licence with the handbook. This edition adds calibration, hand sessions, a metronome and quantise, MIDI files, strike dynamics, a mixer with reverb, key-mapping presets, ghost-hand hints, and songs made from your own music files.

Only the last two dates come from the repository. The stages before them are grouped by what was added, not dated.

03 — What it does

Everything your hands can control

Play

Six modes

Notes, Chords, Drums, Band, Custom (your own sound on every sign) and Song, where a song sheet chooses every sound.

Shape

Loudness and pitch

A quick, snappy sign plays louder than a slow one. Raise your hand for volume, or move it while holding a sign to bend the pitch by up to two semitones.

Sound

A synth for each hand

Ten instruments made live from sine waves, from piano to flute, chosen separately for each hand, with its own volume and reverb.

Record

Loop and MIDI

Stack loop layers over a metronome with count-in and quantise. Send everything to a DAW over MIDI, or save it as a MIDI file.

Game

Charts from your music

Add an MP3, WAV or MIDI file: it finds the tempo, the first bar, the key, the chords and the tune, and writes a chart to play along to.

See

Colours for notes

The room behind you can become a flowing colour field, with each note in Scriabin’s colour for it, blooming as you play.

04 — Under the hood

From a hand to a sound

Every frame follows the same path. The camera thread keeps only the newest image, MediaPipe locates 21 points on each hand, and the code turns finger angles into a sign. The sign becomes a small musical event such as “note on, D4, strength 0.85, right hand”, and that one event goes to the synthesizer, the MIDI port and the looper at the same time.

The tricky part is making a sign feel solid. A finger half-way between up and down would flicker, so each finger has two lines instead of one, and a new sign must be seen in two frames before it plays. Try it below.

2Locate 21 pointsMediaPipe

4Map it to a soundgesture_music.py

Figure A — Try it: one finger, two lines

The finger reads UP

Drag the slider. Going down, the finger only flips to DOWN past the second line; coming back up, it stays DOWN until it crosses the first. In between it keeps whatever it was, so a half-bent finger never flickers.

Bend is the total of a finger’s three joint angles. 90° and 140° are the defaults; calibration fits both lines to each of your fingers (the second set is the handbook’s worked example).

Within 100 ms of the target is Perfect, within 220 ms is Good, anything later or earlier is a Miss. The game times the frame where the sign first appeared, so the confirmation frame does not count against you.

If your camera is consistently late, a timing offset (the `.` key, or --game-offset) shifts the window. Numbers from the handbook’s rhythm-game page.

Ideas and research

  • One Euro filter: G. Casiez, N. Roussel and D. Vogel, “1€ Filter: A Simple Speed-based Low-pass Filter for Noisy Input in Interactive Systems”, CHI 2012. Smooths the hand height.
  • Beat tracking: D. P. W. Ellis, “Beat Tracking by Dynamic Programming”, Journal of New Music Research 36(1), 2007. Used to find the beats in your songs.
  • Key profiles: C. L. Krumhansl and E. J. Kessler, Psychological Review 89(4), 1982. Used to detect a song’s key.
  • Freeverb: the public-domain reverb design by Jezar at Dreampoint. The reverb follows its comb and all-pass structure.
  • Colours for notes: after Alexander Scriabin’s colour scheme for the clavier à lumières in Prometheus: The Poem of Fire (1910). Background: chromesthesia.
  • Music analysis: spectral-flux onsets, chroma features and template chord recognition with Viterbi smoothing, implemented from their textbook descriptions.
  • General MIDI for instrument and drum numbers, and loopMIDI by Tobias Erichsen as the virtual cable into a DAW.

The code

Gesture Music’s own code is MIT-licensed, © 2026 rayangmendrofa. It runs on Windows with Python 3.13 and a webcam, and has a camera-free demo mode.
